### 1. Which "ECS Pains" Does This Get Rid Of?

If you have built complex games with traditional ECS (like Flecs, EnTT, or Unity DOTS), you know that while ECS is amazing for moving 100,000 bullets on screen, it becomes **an absolute nightmare** the moment you try to write rich, nuanced gameplay logic. 

Here are the specific pains this architecture eliminates:

#### Pain 1: "Tag Component Hell" & Fragmented State Machines
* **The ECS Pain:** How do you do a 3-step attack in ECS? You add an `IsAttackingTag`. In the next system, you remove that and add `WaitingForHitTag`. In another system, you check `DamageAppliedTag`. Your game logic gets shattered across 5 different systems across 5 frames. Tracing a bug feels like detective work.
* **How This Cures It:** Your **Domain POD** holds the exact State Machine (`Draft -> InProgress -> Recovering`). The **Monadic Pipeline** executes the entire transition in *one deterministic call*. The logic stays in one readable file, reading top-to-bottom.

#### Pain 2: Multi-Entity Transactions & The "Rollback" Problem
* **The ECS Pain:** Player A trades an item to Player B. 
  1. System 1 removes the item from Player A.
  2. System 2 tries to add it to Player B, but Player B's inventory is full!
  3. Now you have to write clumsy "rollback logic" or deferred command buffers to give the item back to Player A on the next frame.
* **How This Cures It:** The **Saga Orchestrator + `.or_else()`**.
  ```cpp
  trade_pipeline(ctx)
      .and_then(remove_from_player_a)
      .and_then(add_to_player_b)       // If this fails...
      .or_else(rollback_player_a);     // Instant in-frame rollback!
  ```
  It guarantees that gameplay transactions are atomic. No broken inventories, no duplicated items, zero frame delays.

#### Pain 3: Network Rollback (GGPO) & Save/Load Nightmares
* **The ECS Pain:** In fighting games or deterministic multiplayer (RTS), rollback netcode requires you to roll the world back 5 frames and re-simulate. In traditional mutable ECS, deep-cloning dynamic archetype pools is slow and full of edge-case bugs.
* **How This Cures It:** Because your Domain State is **strictly POD** and transitions are **Pure Reducers**, snapshotting is literally a `memcpy` of your flat structs. Re-simulating is just feeding the queue of actions back through the pure pipeline. It makes deterministic multiplayer netcode trivial.

#### Pain 4: "Where on Earth is the Logic?" (Codebase Spaghetti)
* **The ECS Pain:** A junior developer asks: *"What happens when a player drinks a potion?"* In pure ECS, there is no single answer. The logic is smeared across an `InputSystem`, `InventorySystem`, `HealthSystem`, `BuffSystem`, and `AudioSystem`.
* **How This Cures It:** A single pipeline tells the entire story:
  ```cpp
  consume_potion
      .and_then(check_cooldown)
      .and_then(deduct_item)
      .and_then(apply_healing)
      .transform(emit_drinking_events);
  ```
  Anyone on your team can read and understand the entire business rule in 5 seconds.

### 3. The Honest Engineering Trade-Offs (What It *Doesn't* Solve)

To be a true architect, you must know where a pattern does *not* fit:

1. **Spatial Queries & Physics:**
   * If you are doing continuous collision detection (CCD) or hierarchical bounding box (BVH) updates, you need raw spatial trees (R-Trees, Octrees, Kd-Trees) and pointer manipulation. Modeling spatial tree traversal as pure monadic reducers will feel awkward and introduce overhead.
2. **Dynamic Runtime Archetypes:**
   * In a pure ECS like Flecs, you can dynamically slap an arbitrary component onto an entity at runtime (`entity.add<Burning>()`).
   * In strict POD/DOD, dynamic schemas require explicit modeling (like fixed arrays of status effects, bitmasks, or `std::variant`). You trade runtime wildness for strict compile-time type safety.
